How This Bracket Demo Works

You scanned a QR code on a March Madness bracket demo running on Red Hat OpenShift. Here's exactly what it does — and what it doesn't.

🍪

👁️

No Tracking

No analytics. No tracking pixels. No fingerprinting. We don't know who you are and we don't want to.

📡

No Google Analytics, no Facebook pixel, no ad networks. The only external data comes from ESPN and NCAA.com public APIs — through our backend, not your browser.

🔒

The bracket page is display-only. It shows live tournament scores and news from a one-way stream. It can't access anything on your device.

🏀

What does the bracket demo actually do?

It's a live March Madness bracket that pulls real-time scores and headlines from ESPN and NCAA.com through a backend proxy running on Red Hat OpenShift. You can click teams, view game details, and watch scores update — all powered by the same enterprise platform that runs production workloads for the world's largest organizations.

How it works

1

Live data flows from ESPN and NCAA.com

Public APIs from ESPN and NCAA.com provide real-time scores, headlines, game summaries, and bracket structure. These are the same feeds that power sports apps on your phone.

2

Red Hat OpenShift aggregates and caches

A backend proxy on our managed Kubernetes cluster fetches, caches, and normalizes the data. It polls every 30 seconds for scores and pushes updates to all connected browsers via Server-Sent Events — no refreshing required.

3

Your browser shows the bracket in real time

The bracket page is static HTML hosted on GitHub Pages. It opens a one-way connection to our OpenShift cluster and receives score updates as they happen. The ticker scrolls, the scores change, the bracket fills in — all live.

4

Same architecture, different events

Replace "ESPN scores" with "factory sensors" or "building access events" and the architecture is identical. CloudEvents in, processing in the middle, real-time visualization out. The demo is the reference architecture.
